For several years the people of Rostov have been debating the city's traffic congestion. At the Moscow Urban Forum in December 2013, Rostov urbanist Mihail Veklenko met Enrique Peñalosa, one of the world's leading experts on transforming urban environments and transport systems, to find out how the world's major cities tackle this problem.

In his three years as mayor of Bogotá, Enrique Peñalosa completely transformed the city and made it work for the majority of its residents. Instead of building an expensive metro, investing in motorways and expanding car parks, he created pedestrian zones, cycle lanes, schools and libraries, and renewed parks, squares and embankments. He managed to cut traffic in the Colombian capital by 40% with the help of the TransMilenio public transport system, which is rightly considered one of the best in the world.

Today Enrique Peñalosa chairs the board of directors of the Institute for Transportation and Development Policy (ITDP) in New York, advises the governments of a dozen cities in Asia, Africa, Australia, Latin America and the United States, and gives talks and lectures around the world.

## Pedestrians versus drivers

Rostov is currently reconstructing its old tram lines. The important context is the active work on a metro project and the city's role as a host of the 2018 FIFA World Cup. Residents and the city administration acknowledge the transport problems, which, it is assumed, will be solved by building a metro. On closer inspection, however, this is an unjustifiably expensive solution that will take many years. The first metro line alone is estimated to cost 75–100 billion roubles. By comparison, estimates for the first stage put the cost of building the main light rail (LRT) lines by 2018 — from the centre of Rostov-on-Don to the Western and Northern residential districts (ZZhM and SZhM), to the eastern part of the city (Aleksandrovka) and across to the left bank of the Don and the stadium — at 25 billion roubles. This includes the cost of flyovers separating road traffic from the light rail trains, without reducing the number of traffic lanes for cars. The funding could also come as part of the city's preparations for the 2018 World Cup. Rostov residents have put forward a number of comprehensive proposals for reorganising traffic flows, and various options are being discussed by the city administration.

**Mihail Veklenko: Tell us about your approach to the balanced development of public transport. How can Rostov use Bogotá's successful experience?**

We can't solve traffic problems with rail transport alone. In Bogotá, people want a metro: it is fast, underground and off the roads. But what people actually want is not the metro as such, but its qualities: speed, convenience, safety. Well-organised surface transport can deliver far better results in terms of convenience, speed and accessibility. Buses are a powerful symbol of democracy. When a bus with a hundred passengers moves along its dedicated lane while cars are stuck in traffic — that is democracy in action. We shouldn't put people underground like rats; that is terrible.

There are cases where buses work more efficiently than trains and trams. In Spain, buses bring more passengers into Madrid every day than two railway lines running from the same place. A bus can get close to where people live or work and let them board and alight conveniently. Its greatest advantage, though, is that on the trunk route it moves as fast as a train, and in most cases passengers don't need to transfer.

**Mihail Veklenko: If, as you say, buses win on so many counts, why do the world's leading cities still give priority to rail?**

The reason is simple: the world's first metro was built in the 1860s and 1870s, while buses have only been in use since 1935, when tyres became strong enough to carry such loads. Traffic jams can be tackled not only by adding roads and improving public transport, but also by restricting car traffic. In modern cities, the first step in this direction is restricting parking in the city centre. Parking is not an individual's constitutional right — there is no country in the world where it is. Just as you decide for yourself where to keep your belongings and your food, you should decide for yourself where to park. You don't go to the city administration and ask where to keep your things, your clothes and your food, do you? This is not a technical question but a political one, so it calls for a political decision. And you, as citizens, have to decide: is your city for drivers or for pedestrians? Will you give priority to widening roads or pavements — to more space for cars or for people on foot?

How public space is prioritised is one of the most important and most contested issues in most cities of the world today. It concerns both the priority of public transport over private cars and the provision of public spaces, parks and places to relax in the city — for people, instead of roads and car parks. This conflict is especially visible in developing countries, where pedestrians and drivers are often engaged in a real struggle for space. The very existence of this conflict shows that people who walk are treated as third-class citizens, while those who drive are first-class citizens. In reality, when it comes to transport infrastructure, the difference between advanced and backward cities is not whether they have enough roads or metro lines — it depends far more on the quality of their pavements and pedestrian areas.

## Respect for human nature

At the moment buses are not a priority in Rostov. After private cars, the main mode of transport is the small minibus (the *marshrutka*). On one of the main thoroughfares, Krasnoarmeyskaya Street, there are no buses at all, and on the streets that do have buses, passengers have to wait at the stop for up to ten minutes while the driver fills the bus. This makes for very uncomfortable conditions, and naturally people prefer the faster minibuses, which sometimes even stop on request. Yet the sheer number of minibuses causes congestion on the roads.

**Mihail Veklenko: In your view, what measures could address these problems and get the existing transport system working as efficiently as possible?**

You can solve this by changing the way operators are paid. Instead of paying them for the number of passengers carried, you should pay them for the number of kilometres covered per shift — and perhaps pay a bonus for the number of passengers only as an additional incentive. This leads to better-organised urban public transport and higher speeds. Another important point is that a bus system is more flexible: it can be reconfigured as the city changes, and routes can be modified or added. It makes sense to combine your existing trams and the new rapid trams you are planning with a bus rapid transit system.

But remember that any transport system is only part of the job. The main thing is to understand that people vitally need urban space to walk in. Places to relax in the city are more important than shops and entertainment in shopping malls. Walking, and enjoying a walk through the city, is a natural human need. For one of our public awareness campaigns in Bogotá we came up with this slogan: "Just as birds need to fly, fish need to swim and bear cubs need to run, people need to walk. The city must respect human nature."

**Mihail Veklenko: In another campaign video, about the fight to win pavements back for pedestrians, a group of people played cards in the middle of a busy road, at risk of being hit by a car. What did you want to show about the balance between transport solutions and urban space in Bogotá?**

The slogan of that campaign was: "Just as people should not be in the space for cars, cars should not be in the space for people." Any city that wants its citizens to be happy must have public spaces, boulevards, promenades, cycle lanes and pavements that are pleasant to walk along. Bogotá, for example, is a city with enormous poverty. Around a million people there have no home of their own. We have to be very careful with the little money there is in the budget. In our situation it would simply be immoral to invest in metro lines to solve traffic congestion when the city has so many other problems: a shortage of social housing, health care, education and many other areas of life. Moscow, on the other hand, is a rich city: it can afford to invest in extensive road and metro construction and still have money left in the budget.

At the same time, the state can give its residents simple but extremely important things that no corporation can provide. Most of what the state does is about necessities. Parks and public spaces simply make you happy. That is the miracle of public spaces and pedestrian areas: they are created not for economic development, but for your leisure and a better quality of life.

## Urban space is a valuable resource

Mr Peñalosa is convinced that cities today need to reduce the space given to cars and, where pavements have been used for parking for many years, to free them up for pedestrians and for safe cycle lanes. He points to the world's largest cities as examples.

**Mihail Veklenko: Enrique, you often speak about urban mobility. What is it, and how does it show itself?**

Mobility is a question of how road space is distributed. That is what determines the speed of a city. Ever since the car appeared, cities have been built with more attention to mobility than to the comfort of the people who live in them. Unlike health care, education or housing, it is not always obvious that mobility and the urban environment have an enormous impact on people's quality of life.

As societies grow richer, mobility gets worse. Like most other problems of developing countries, mobility is not only a matter of money or technology — it is above all a question of equality and justice. We have become so used to the great inequality in developing countries that we no longer notice it. An advanced city is one where people from all walks of life use public transport or bicycles, not just cars. In Amsterdam, for example, more than thirty per cent of the population get around the city by bicycle.

In urban development there is competition for public investment: the more money goes into highways, the less goes into housing, schools and hospitals. There is also a conflict between drivers and pedestrians over space in the city. Today it is generally accepted that private property and the market economy are the best ways to manage resources. But a market economy needs income inequality in order to function. Some people earn more, others less. Some companies succeed while others fail.

**Mihail Veklenko: Then the question is: what kind of equality can we ensure in a market economy today? What should it be based on, and how does it relate to a city's transport system?**

Beyond the obvious — health care and education — all children should have access to green spaces, sports facilities and swimming pools. In our society, children are the indicator of well-being. And yet thousands of children are killed by cars. It is time to build cities for children: then they will be good places to live for everyone else too.

The second kind of equality is what we might call "democratic equality". The first article of almost every constitution states that all citizens are equal before the law. This is not just poetry; it is a very powerful principle. It means that a bus carrying a hundred passengers is entitled to a hundred times more road space than a car carrying one. The democratic principle in action is when the public good prevails over private interests — while still taking every individual's interests into account.

Today we have to accept that cities need to reduce the space given to cars and, where pavements have been used for parking for many years, free them up for pedestrians and for safe cycling. We can see such solutions in Paris, Berlin, New York, London, Bogotá, Moscow and other cities. In every one of them there were difficulties in how different groups of users perceived the changes. But once the tipping point was passed, people came to understand that public space belongs to the majority. A city should reflect respect for human dignity and show that drivers and pedestrians have equal rights. And so we arrive at an ideological and political question that matters for any city: how should we distribute the most valuable resource — urban space — between pedestrians, cyclists, public transport and cars? This is not a technological problem but a political one, and we should remember that no constitution in the world mentions a right to parking.

## About Enrique Peñalosa

Enrique Peñalosa (Colombia) was mayor of Bogotá from 1998 to 2001 and was elected to the office again in 2015, serving a second term from 1 January 2016. During his first term he created a new urban environment in Bogotá, with parks and pedestrian areas; one of his main achievements in 1998–2001 was a 40% reduction in city traffic. Peñalosa has also worked as a journalist and as a consultant on urban and transport policy.

He was born on 30 September 1954 in Washington, D.C., and grew up in Colombia. He studied first in the United States and then in France, earning a degree in economics and history and later an MBA. While studying, he worked as a dishwasher in a restaurant and as a bellboy in a hotel. He then returned to Bogotá, where he began his public career while also working for the city's public water company. In 1986 he became an economic adviser to the President of Colombia. In 1990 he was elected to Congress, and in 1991 he stood for mayor of Bogotá. He lost that election and the next one in 1994, but won in 1997.

Peñalosa's main achievements include reviving Bogotá's old parks and creating several new ones, renovating the city's most important avenues, and building one of the best transport systems in the world, TransMilenio, which made the 40% traffic reduction possible. His transport policy gives public transport priority over private cars: some streets in the city are closed to cars on principle and are used only by buses, cyclists and pedestrians. He also reformed the policy on informal street trading, which had taken over the city's streets and public spaces, and cleared cars off the pavements to give more space to pedestrians.

Enrique Peñalosa chairs the board of directors of the Institute for Transportation and Development Policy (ITDP) in New York, advises governments in Asia, Africa, Australia, Latin America and the United States, and speaks at environmental, urban and university forums. In 2014 he ran for president of Colombia, and in 2015 he was again elected mayor of Bogotá.
